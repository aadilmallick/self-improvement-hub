I have started roughly nine "personal knowledge systems". Two survived. Both of
them had the same property: they were organized by **what the note is for**, not
by when I wrote it.

{/* truncate */}

The graveyard looked like this: a notebook with 300 highlights, a Notion
workspace with a beautiful database, a folder called `inbox` that had not been
empty since 2023. None of it was useful, and all of it felt like progress.

## The failure wasn't the tool

Every abandoned system died at the same point: the note existed, but there was no
next action attached to it. A highlight is a bookmark for a thought I never had.

So the rule I'm using here is simple. A note earns its place if it changes one of
three things:

1. **What I do tomorrow** — a protocol, a checklist, a habit.
2. **How I decide** — a model that resolves a recurring question.
3. **What I make** — raw material for an essay or a project.

If it changes none of those, it gets deleted at the next review. That's the whole
quality bar, and it is much lower than the one I used to apply — which is why it
actually holds.

## Organized by pillar, not by date

The hub is grouped into five pillars: foundations, systems, body & energy,
learning, and a toolkit of templates. Inside each one, notes are edited forever.
They are not posts. If a note is wrong in six months, I fix it rather than
writing a follow-up that contradicts it.

Essays are the opposite. They are dated and frozen — a snapshot of what I thought
in October 2026, not a claim that it's still right.

That split fixed a problem I didn't know I had: I was using one format for two
different jobs, so everything was slightly out of date and nothing was ever
finished.

## What lives where

- **Notes** answer a question permanently. They get references to each other and links from the review.
- **Essays** answer a question once. They are allowed to be wrong later.
- **Templates** are the parts you'd actually copy. If a note is good, it usually produces one.

The templates are the test of whether any of this is real. If I read something
and can't produce a checklist, an experiment, or a paragraph from it, I probably
didn't understand it well enough to keep it.

## The loop still matters more than the library

Building this site could become its own form of procrastination — a very pleasant
one, with fonts. So the rule for the site itself is the same rule as for
everything else: **the review is the product.**

Thirty minutes on Sunday. Three numbers. One priority. One experiment. If the hub
stops feeding that loop, it stops being a hub and becomes a museum.
