# Focus & dopamine

This is the note I reread most often. Most "I have no discipline" problems are
actually **attention and reward problems**, and they respond to design far better
than to guilt.

## Wanting is not liking

The popular story is that dopamine is the pleasure chemical. The more useful
model: dopamine is the **anticipation and pursuit** system. It generates the pull
toward something *before* you have it.

- **Wanting** — dopamine. Drive, focus, restlessness, "just one more".
- **Liking** — opioids and endorphins. The actual felt enjoyment of the thing.

This split explains the most confusing experience in modern life: craving
something intensely and feeling flat the moment you get it. The pursuit system
fired; the enjoyment system barely showed up.

## Reward prediction error

Dopamine neurons respond most strongly to **surprise**, not to reward itself.

```
RPE = actual reward − expected reward
```

| Scenario | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Better than expected | Spike above baseline — "remember what we just did" |
| Exactly as expected | Flat — nothing new to learn |
| Worse than expected | Dip below baseline — restlessness, letdown |

This is why a predictable reward stops motivating you, and why **unpredictable**
rewards are so sticky.

## The slot machine in your pocket

Infinite feeds run a variable-ratio schedule: you never know whether the next
swipe is boring or brilliant. That uncertainty keeps anticipation elevated and
makes the behaviour compulsive. Two extra effects make it worse:

1. **Cheap rewards train expensive expectations.** When your brain calibrates to high-stimulation input, a book or a spreadsheet feels like a negative prediction error.
2. **The willpower center fatigues.** Micro-decisions accumulate; you keep scrolling not because it is fun but because stopping requires one more decision than continuing.

## Tolerance and the flat baseline

Repeated super-stimulation pushes the system toward downregulation: fewer
available receptors, a lower tonic baseline, less interest in ordinary things.
The subjective version is: *everything I used to enjoy now requires effort.*

Recovery is not mystical, but it is not instant either. Expect two to four weeks
of reduced input before ordinary rewards start registering properly again. Most
people quit on day five, when it feels worse, and conclude they are broken.

## The reset protocols

1. **Morning delay.** No feed or notifications for the first 30 minutes after waking. You are protecting the baseline before the day prices it.
2. **Separate spaces.** Phone in another room during deep work. Not face-down on the desk — **another room**. Presence is a cue.
3. **A defined fast.** Pick a length you will actually finish: 7, 14, or 30 days of no infinite feeds. Write down what is included before you start.
4. **Urge surfing.** When the pull arrives, do nothing for ten minutes and watch the physical sensation. Cravings are waves with a peak and an end; the skill is not fighting them, it is not obeying them.
5. **Boring rewards.** Replace stimulation with something slower: walking, reading, cooking, an actual conversation. The point is to re-teach the system that low-intensity input counts.

:::warning[Willpower is a bad primary strategy]

If your plan is "resist the phone", you have designed a plan that spends a
limited resource all day. Move the phone, log out, use a blocker, leave the
device charging in another room. Friction over force.

:::

## A honest self-audit

Answer these, then pick **one** change:

- What is the first thing I touch in the morning, and the last thing at night?
- Which apps do I open without deciding to?
- When did I last do something boring for thirty minutes without reaching for input?
- What am I avoiding when I reach for the phone? (The answer is usually a feeling, not a task.)
